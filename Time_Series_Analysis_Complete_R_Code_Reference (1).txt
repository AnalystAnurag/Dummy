INTERPRETATION NOTES – NORMAL TIME SERIES ANALYSIS
--------------------------------------------------
- A time plot is an exploratory tool. Look for trend, seasonality, structural changes, outliers and changing variance.
- A log transformation is suitable only when observations are positive. Differences in log values approximate proportional/percentage changes for small movements.
- In additive decomposition, observed = trend + seasonal + irregular. In multiplicative decomposition, observed = trend × seasonal × irregular.
- A seasonal index above the additive baseline indicates a month/period that tends to be above the trend; below the baseline indicates a negative seasonal contribution.
- Moving averages smooth short-term fluctuations. A longer window produces a smoother series but reacts more slowly to changes.
- Least-squares trend estimates a fitted deterministic trend over the sample. Semi-averages provide a simple two-period trend approximation.
- `skewness()` describes asymmetry: positive values indicate right-skew; negative values indicate left-skew.
- `kurtosis()` interpretation depends on the package's definition. For `moments::kurtosis`, a value around 3 is approximately normal-like; above 3 is leptokurtic and below 3 is platykurtic.
- Do not infer stationarity from a plot alone. Use formal tests such as ADF and KPSS in the ARIMA/VAR sections.

$$
\begin{aligned}
  H_0 &: \text{The time series is non-stationary (contains a unit root)} \\
  H_1 &: \text{The time series is stationary}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Interpretation:

### Performing stationary test using ADF test

$$ 
\begin{aligned}
H_0&: \text{Data is stationary} \\ 
H_1&: \text{Data is non stationary}
\end{aligned}
$$


### Performing VECM test

```{r}
vecm_model <- cajorls(jo_test, r =1)
summary(vecm_model$rlm)
```
#### Interpretation: 

** The VECM results indicate that the long-run equilibrium relationship is statistically significant for four of the five sectors. The error-correction coefficients for Healthcare (−0.000108, p < 0.001), Financial Services (−0.000207, p < 0.001), Auto (−0.000297, p < 0.001), and Bank (−0.000559, p < 0.001) are negative and statistically significant, indicating that these sectors adjust toward the long-run equilibrium following a deviation. Among these sectors, Banking exhibits the largest absolute adjustment coefficient, suggesting the strongest adjustment response, followed by Auto, Financial Services and Healthcare. In contrast, the error-correction coefficient for FMCG is statistically insignificant (p = 0.832), indicating that FMCG does not significantly adjust to restore the long-run equilibrium. The short-run dynamics are comparatively limited, with significant lagged effects primarily involving Healthcare, Financial Services and Banking.

### Performing VAR test

```{r}
VAR1 <- VAR(sec_y, lag.max =1, type = "const",
            season = NULL , exog = NULL)
summary(VAR1)
```
```{r}
stargazer(VAR1[["varresult"]], type = "text")
```
#### Interpretation: 

** The estimated VAR(1) model is stable, meaning that shocks to the system are not expected to persist indefinitely and the model is suitable for further VAR-based analysis such as impulse response and forecast error variance decomposition.

** In the Healthcare equation, only the lagged Healthcare return is statistically significant (β = 0.0677, p < 0.01), indicating limited short-run persistence in Healthcare returns. Lagged returns of FMCG, Financial Services, Auto and Bank do not have statistically significant effects on current Healthcare returns.

** There is no statistically significant evidence that the previous day's returns of any of the five sectors explain current FMCG returns.

** The Financial Services equation shows significant short-run dependence on lagged Healthcare and Banking returns. Lagged Healthcare returns have a negative association with current Financial Services returns (β = −0.0659, p = 0.0119), while lagged Banking returns have a positive association (β = 0.1920, p = 0.0039). The lagged FS return itself is marginally significant at the 10% level.

** Current Auto returns show weak evidence of dependence on lagged Financial Services returns, with a positive coefficient of 0.1155 (p = 0.0904). However, this relationship is only marginally significant at the 10% level.

** The Banking equation indicates significant dependence on lagged Healthcare and Banking returns. Lagged Healthcare returns have a negative association with current Banking returns (β = −0.0711, p = 0.0100), while lagged Banking returns have a positive association (β = 0.1893, p = 0.0069), indicating short-run persistence in Banking returns.

** Overall, the results suggest that cross-sector interdependence is stronger within the same trading period than through one-day lagged return transmission.

============================================================
INTERPRETATION NOTES – GARCH MODELS
============================================================

1. DATA IMPORT, CLEANING AND PRICE PLOTS
- Confirm that the date index is correctly parsed, observations are chronological, and price columns are numeric.
- Check that all prices are positive before calculating log returns.
- Remove or investigate missing values; do not silently remove large blocks of data without checking their cause.

2. RAW-SERIES STATIONARITY
- ADF null hypothesis: the series has a unit root (non-stationary).
- ADF p-value below 0.05: reject the unit-root null; evidence supports stationarity.
- ADF p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the unit-root null; insufficient evidence of stationarity.
- Price/index levels are often non-stationary. This does not by itself mean the return series is non-stationary.

3. RETURN SERIES
- `diff(log(price))` gives continuously compounded returns; multiply by 100 if percentage units are preferred.
- Log returns are approximately percentage changes for small movements.
- Confirm that all return series use the same dates before combining them.

4. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS AND DISTRIBUTION
- Positive skewness indicates a longer/right tail; negative skewness indicates a longer/left tail.
- Kurtosis above 3 (under the conventional Pearson definition) indicates heavier tails than a normal distribution; below 3 indicates lighter tails.
- Financial returns often exhibit fat tails, so a normality rejection is common and can motivate a Student-t innovation distribution.
- Check the package's kurtosis convention before interpreting the benchmark value.

5. RETURN-SERIES STATIONARITY
- ADF p-value below 0.05 supports stationarity of returns.
- A stationary mean does not imply constant variance. Volatility can still change over time.

6. VOLATILITY CLUSTERING
- Volatility clustering means large absolute movements tend to be followed by large movements, and small movements by small movements.
- A return plot can reveal clustering, but formal ARCH testing is needed to assess conditional heteroskedasticity.

7. ARCH EFFECT TEST
- ARCH-LM null hypothesis: no ARCH effects (no conditional heteroskedasticity up to the tested lag order).
- p-value below 0.05: reject the null; evidence of ARCH effects. A conditional-volatility model may be appropriate.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null; the test does not detect ARCH effects at the chosen lag order.

8. SERIAL CORRELATION TEST
- Ljung-Box null hypothesis: no autocorrelation up to the selected lag.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null; no significant serial correlation detected at the tested lags.
- p-value below 0.05: evidence of remaining serial correlation. Consider whether the mean equation needs ARMA terms.
- Test returns and standardized residuals separately; they answer different diagnostic questions.

9. NORMALITY TEST
- Jarque-Bera null hypothesis: skewness and kurtosis are consistent with normality.
- p-value below 0.05: reject normality.
- Non-normal returns do not automatically rule out GARCH. Consider an appropriate innovation distribution, such as Student-t, and inspect standardized residuals.

10. sGARCH(1,1) MODEL
- The conditional variance equation includes a constant, a lagged squared innovation (ARCH effect) and a lagged conditional variance (GARCH effect).
- `alpha1` measures the response of volatility to recent shocks.
- `beta1` measures volatility persistence.
- `alpha1 + beta1` close to 1 indicates highly persistent volatility; a value below 1 is commonly associated with covariance-stationary variance under standard conditions.
- Check parameter constraints and convergence before interpreting estimates.
- A significant ARCH parameter indicates that recent shocks help explain current volatility; a significant GARCH parameter indicates persistence.

11. MEAN EQUATION
- ARMA terms model serial dependence in the conditional mean.
- Compare mean specifications using diagnostics and information criteria, not just coefficient significance.
- The mean equation and variance equation serve different purposes.

12. MODEL DIAGNOSTICS
- Standardized residuals should have no substantial remaining autocorrelation.
- Ljung-Box test on standardized residuals: p-value above 0.05 is consistent with no detected serial correlation.
- ARCH-LM test on standardized residuals: p-value above 0.05 is consistent with no remaining ARCH effect.
- Inspect residual ACF and diagnostic plots as well as test p-values.
- A good volatility model should capture conditional heteroskedasticity without leaving substantial structure in standardized residuals.

13. VOLATILITY FORECASTING
- `ugarchforecast()` produces forecasts of conditional mean and/or conditional variance.
- The conditional sigma forecast represents the modelled volatility, not the forecasted asset price.
- Volatility forecasts are conditional on the fitted model and generally revert toward the model's long-run variance when the model is mean-reverting.
- Clearly state the forecast horizon and the units used for returns/volatility.

14. EGARCH AND GJR-GARCH
- EGARCH models log conditional variance and can capture asymmetric responses to positive and negative shocks.
- GJR-GARCH adds an asymmetric term so negative and positive shocks can have different volatility effects.
- In these models, interpret the asymmetry parameter according to the exact parameterisation and software output; do not assume the sign has the same meaning across every implementation.
- Compare alternative specifications using information criteria, convergence, parameter plausibility and standardized-residual diagnostics.

15. DCC-GARCH
- DCC-GARCH models time-varying conditional correlations between multiple return series.
- `rcor()` extracts dynamic conditional correlations; `rcov()` extracts conditional covariance matrices.
- Correlations near +1 indicate strong positive co-movement; near -1 indicate strong negative co-movement; near 0 indicates weak linear co-movement.
- A changing correlation series indicates that diversification relationships vary over time.
- DCC estimates are conditional on the chosen univariate volatility specifications and model assumptions.

16. BEKK-GARCH
- BEKK models conditional covariance dynamics and can represent volatility and shock spillovers across series.
- Interpret the estimated matrices, t-statistics and p-values according to the package's parameterisation.
- A statistically significant off-diagonal element may provide evidence of cross-market shock/volatility transmission within the fitted model.
- Statistical significance is not, by itself, proof of economic causality.

GENERAL MODEL-SELECTION AND REPORTING REMINDERS
============================================================
- ARIMA is primarily for univariate forecasting.
- VAR models short-run interactions among stationary variables.
- VECM is appropriate for commonly I(1) variables with one or more cointegrating relationships.
- GARCH models time-varying conditional variance/volatility.

# Time-Series Econometrics: ARIMA, VAR, VECM and GARCH
## Step-by-step methodology, purpose of each step, and interpretation

**Purpose:** This guide explains the workflow for four time-series modelling approaches: ARIMA, VAR, VECM and GARCH. For every step, it explains (1) what we do, (2) why we do it, and (3) how to interpret the result.

**Important distinction:** ARIMA is primarily used for univariate forecasting; VAR models short-run interactions among multiple variables; VECM models short-run dynamics while incorporating long-run cointegrating relationships; GARCH models time-varying conditional volatility. They answer different research questions and are not interchangeable.

---

# 1. ARIMA – Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

## 1.1 Objective

ARIMA is a univariate time-series model. It uses the past values and past forecast errors of one variable to explain its current value and generate forecasts.

An ARIMA model is written as:

\[
ARIMA(p,d,q)
\]

- **p – AR order:** Number of lagged values of the series.
- **d – Integration order:** Number of times the series is differenced to achieve stationarity.
- **q – MA order:** Number of lagged forecast errors included in the model.

Use ARIMA when the main objective is to understand and forecast one series, such as an index price, sales, demand or output.

## 1.2 Step-by-step methodology

### Step 1: Import and understand the data

**What we do**
- Import the time-series data.
- Identify the date/time variable and the value to be modelled.
- Check data type, frequency, date range, missing observations and duplicate dates.
- Sort observations chronologically.

**Why we do it**

Time-series methods depend on the correct time order. Incorrect date formats, unsorted observations, duplicate dates or missing values can produce misleading results.

**How to interpret**

- The date range tells us the sample period.
- The observation count indicates the amount of available information.
- A numeric variable should be stored as numeric, not as text.
- Check whether the series is daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly or annual.

### Step 2: Plot the original series

**What we do**

Plot the observed series against time.

**Why we do it**

The plot provides an initial view of trend, seasonality, structural changes, outliers and changing variance.

**How to interpret**

- A persistent upward or downward movement suggests a trend.
- A repeating pattern at a fixed interval suggests seasonality.
- Sudden level changes may indicate structural breaks or unusual events.
- Increasing fluctuation over time may indicate changing variance.

A plot is an exploratory tool; it does not formally establish stationarity.

### Step 3: Transform the series if required

**What we do**

Consider a logarithmic transformation when the series is positive and variability increases with its level.

\[
Y_t^* = \log(Y_t)
\]

**Why we do it**

A log transformation can stabilise variance and make changes interpretable in proportional terms.

**How to interpret**

Differences in log values approximate percentage changes for small movements:

\[
\Delta \log(Y_t) \approx \frac{Y_t-Y_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}}
\]

Do not take logarithms of zero or negative values.

### Step 4: Test for stationarity

**What we do**

Use tests such as Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and KPSS.

**Why we do it**

The standard ARIMA identification process is based on obtaining a stationary series after differencing. A stationary series has statistical properties such as mean and variance that are broadly stable over time.

**Hypotheses**

ADF test:

\[
H_0: \text{The series has a unit root (non-stationary)}
\]
\[
H_1: \text{The series is stationary}
\]

KPSS test:

\[
H_0: \text{The series is stationary}
\]
\[
H_1: \text{The series is non-stationary}
\]

**How to interpret**

| Test | p-value | Decision |
|---|---:|---|
| ADF | Below 0.05 | Reject unit-root null; evidence supports stationarity |
| ADF | Above 0.05 | Fail to reject unit-root null; insufficient evidence of stationarity |
| KPSS | Below 0.05 | Reject stationarity null; evidence supports non-stationarity |
| KPSS | Above 0.05 | Fail to reject stationarity null |

The ADF and KPSS tests have opposite null hypotheses. Reading them together provides a more informative assessment. Results can sometimes be inconclusive or conflicting.

### Step 5: Difference the series if required

**What we do**

Apply first differencing:

\[
\Delta Y_t = Y_t-Y_{t-1}
\]

For a log-transformed series:

\[
\Delta \log(Y_t)=\log(Y_t)-\log(Y_{t-1})
\]

If the first difference is still non-stationary, investigate whether a second difference is justified.

**Why we do it**

Differencing removes stochastic trends and helps achieve stationarity.

**How to interpret**

- If the original series is non-stationary but the first difference is stationary, the series is commonly treated as I(1), so d = 1.
- If the original series is already stationary, d = 0.
- Avoid over-differencing. Excessive differencing can introduce unnecessary noise and distort the autocorrelation structure.

### Step 6: Use ACF and PACF to identify candidate orders

**What we do**

Plot the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) of the stationary series.

**Why we do it**

They provide clues about the autoregressive order p and moving-average order q.

**How to interpret**

- **ACF:** Shows correlation between the series and its lagged values.
- **PACF:** Shows the relationship with a particular lag after controlling for shorter lags.
- A PACF cut-off can suggest an AR component.
- An ACF cut-off can suggest an MA component.
- Gradual decay can indicate mixed ARMA behaviour.

These are identification clues, not rigid rules. Consider multiple candidate models.

### Step 7: Estimate candidate ARIMA models

**What we do**

Estimate plausible ARIMA(p,d,q) specifications using the original series and the selected differencing order.

**Why we do it**

Different combinations of p and q may capture the time dependence differently. Estimating candidates allows us to compare model fit.

**How to interpret**

Review coefficient estimates, standard errors, p-values and information criteria. Coefficients should be considered in the context of model specification and diagnostics.

### Step 8: Compare models using AIC and BIC

**What we do**

Compare candidate models using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

**Why we do it**

These criteria balance model fit against model complexity.

**How to interpret**

- Lower AIC is preferred among comparable candidate models.
- Lower BIC is preferred among comparable candidate models.
- BIC generally penalises additional parameters more strongly than AIC.
- Compare models estimated on the same data and under comparable likelihood assumptions.

Do not select a model solely because it has the lowest AIC/BIC.

### Step 9: Check residual diagnostics

**What we do**

Inspect residual plots, residual ACF and a Ljung-Box test. In R, `checkresiduals()` provides a convenient diagnostic summary for many forecast models.

**Why we do it**

A suitable ARIMA model should capture the systematic serial dependence. Residuals should not contain substantial remaining autocorrelation.

**How to interpret**

- Residuals should fluctuate around zero without a clear pattern.
- Residual ACF spikes should mostly remain within the approximate significance bounds.
- Ljung-Box p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null of no residual autocorrelation at the tested lags.
- Ljung-Box p-value below 0.05: evidence of remaining autocorrelation; the model may be inadequate.

A good residual result does not guarantee accurate future forecasts, but it is an important adequacy check.

### Step 10: Evaluate forecast accuracy

**What we do**

Separate the data chronologically into training and test periods, fit the model on training data, and forecast the test period.

Common metrics:

\[
MAE = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}|y_t-\hat y_t|
\]

\[
RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_t-\hat y_t)^2}
\]

**Why we do it**

In-sample fit can look good even when a model forecasts poorly. A holdout period provides a more realistic assessment.

**How to interpret**

- Lower MAE indicates a lower average absolute error.
- Lower RMSE indicates lower squared-error loss and penalises large errors more strongly.
- Compare models on the same test period and scale.
- MAPE can be unsuitable when actual values are zero or close to zero.

### Step 11: Forecast future values

**What we do**

Refit the selected model using the appropriate available sample and generate forecasts with prediction intervals.

**Why we do it**

Forecasting is the principal objective when ARIMA is used for prediction.

**How to interpret**

- The forecast line is the model's point prediction.
- Prediction intervals represent uncertainty around the forecast.
- Intervals generally widen as the forecast horizon increases.
- For market data, forecast dates should follow trading sessions rather than automatically assuming calendar days.

## 1.3 ARIMA workflow summary

Data import → Plot → Transform if needed → ADF/KPSS → Differencing → ACF/PACF → Estimate candidate models → AIC/BIC comparison → Residual diagnostics → Holdout accuracy → Forecast.

# 2. VAR – Vector Autoregression

## 2.1 Objective

VAR is a multivariate time-series model used to study the dynamic relationships among two or more variables. Each variable is modelled as a function of its own lags and the lags of all other variables in the system.

A VAR(p) model can be represented as:

\[
Y_t = c + A_1Y_{t-1}+A_2Y_{t-2}+\cdots+A_pY_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t
\]

where:
- \(Y_t\) is a vector of endogenous variables.
- \(c\) is a vector of constants.
- \(A_i\) are coefficient matrices.
- p is the lag order.
- \(\varepsilon_t\) is a vector of innovations.

Use VAR when the research question concerns short-run interactions and predictive relationships among multiple stationary variables.

## 2.2 Step-by-step methodology

### Step 1: Select and prepare the variables

**What we do**
- Select the variables relevant to the research question.
- Align all variables to a common time frequency and date index.
- Check missing values, duplicates, data types and outliers.
- Ensure all series cover the same sample period.

**Why we do it**

VAR estimates a joint system. The observations must be aligned so that each row represents the same time period across all variables.

**How to interpret**

A clean multivariate dataset should have consistent dates and no accidental misalignment. A missing date in one series can otherwise create false relationships.

### Step 2: Explore the individual series

**What we do**

Review time plots and descriptive statistics for each variable.

**Why we do it**

This identifies trends, outliers, scale differences, unusual periods and possible data problems before modelling.

**How to interpret**

Look for common movements, volatility episodes and trends. Similar movements do not by themselves establish causality or a dynamic relationship.

### Step 3: Test each series for stationarity

**What we do**

Apply ADF and, where useful, KPSS to each variable.

**Why we do it**

A standard VAR in levels is generally estimated using stationary variables unless a suitable cointegration framework is used.

**How to interpret**

Use the ADF/KPSS null hypotheses explained in the ARIMA section. Do not assume that all variables have the same integration order; test them individually.

### Step 4: Transform non-stationary variables

**What we do**

Depending on the variable and research question, use first differences, log differences or another justified transformation.

For a positive index or price:

\[
r_t = \Delta \log(P_t)
\]

**Why we do it**

Differencing or log differencing can produce stationary growth or return series suitable for a standard VAR.

**How to interpret**

- Log differences are approximately continuously compounded returns.
- First differences represent absolute changes.
- The transformation changes the question: a VAR in returns explains return dynamics, not directly the long-run relationship among price levels.

### Step 5: Combine the stationary series

**What we do**

Bind the transformed variables into one multivariate time-series object.

**Why we do it**

VAR requires a joint system with aligned observations.

**How to interpret**

Check that the column names are correct, observations are aligned and no unexpected missing values remain.

### Step 6: Select the optimal lag length

**What we do**

Use a lag selection procedure such as `VARselect()` and compare AIC, HQ, SC/BIC and FPE.

**Why we do it**

The lag order determines how many past periods influence the current system. Too few lags may omit important dynamics; too many consume degrees of freedom and may reduce estimation precision.

**How to interpret**

- Each information criterion proposes a lag order.
- AIC/FPE may select longer lag structures.
- SC/BIC often favours more parsimonious models.
- Criteria can disagree. Use economic reasoning, sample size and residual diagnostics when deciding.

### Step 7: Estimate the VAR model

**What we do**

Estimate VAR(p) using the selected lag order and a justified deterministic specification, such as a constant.

**Why we do it**

The model estimates how the current value of each variable relates to lagged values of all variables in the system.

**How to interpret**

Each equation has its own dependent variable. A coefficient on a lagged variable describes its conditional association with the current dependent variable, holding other included lagged variables constant.

A statistically significant coefficient indicates evidence of a lagged association within the model. It does not automatically establish causation.

### Step 8: Check VAR stability

**What we do**

Examine the roots or eigenvalues of the VAR companion matrix.

**Why we do it**

Stability is important for meaningful dynamic analysis, impulse responses and forecast behaviour.

**How to interpret**

For a stable VAR, all companion-matrix root moduli should be below 1. In R, the interpretation of `roots()` output depends on the function's parameterisation; when requesting `modulus = TRUE`, values below 1 indicate stability.

### Step 9: Test residual serial correlation

**What we do**

Use a multivariate Portmanteau or another suitable serial-correlation test.

**Why we do it**

Residual serial correlation suggests the model has not captured all relevant time dependence.

**How to interpret**

- Null: no residual serial correlation up to the tested order.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null.
- p-value below 0.05: evidence of remaining serial correlation; reconsider lag order or specification.

### Step 10: Test for ARCH effects and residual normality

**What we do**

Use multivariate ARCH tests and residual normality tests where appropriate.

**Why we do it**

These tests assess additional residual properties that may matter for inference and volatility modelling.

**How to interpret**

- ARCH test null: no ARCH effects.
- Normality test null: residuals are normally distributed.
- p-value below 0.05 indicates evidence against the relevant null.
- Non-normal residuals do not automatically invalidate every VAR use, but they can affect conventional inference and motivate robust methods.

### Step 11: Check parameter stability over time

**What we do**

Use stability procedures such as OLS-CUSUM and inspect the plots.

**Why we do it**

Relationships may change because of policy changes, crises, market shocks or structural breaks.

**How to interpret**

If the fluctuation process stays within the critical boundaries, there is no detected instability under that test. Crossing the boundaries indicates evidence of instability.

### Step 12: Conduct Granger-causality tests

**What we do**

Test whether the lags of one variable jointly help predict another variable or the remaining system.

**Why we do it**

This assesses predictive content beyond the other included lagged variables.

**Hypotheses**

\[
H_0: \text{The cause variable does not Granger-cause the target/system}
\]

\[
H_1: \text{The cause variable Granger-causes the target/system}
\]

**How to interpret**

- p-value below 0.05: reject the null; evidence of Granger-predictive content.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null.
- Granger causality is not proof of structural, economic or policy causation.

### Step 13: Estimate impulse response functions (IRF)

**What we do**

Estimate the response of variables to shocks in the system over a chosen forecast horizon.

**Why we do it**

IRFs show the direction, size and persistence of dynamic responses after an innovation.

**How to interpret**

- Identify the impulse variable (the variable receiving the shock).
- Identify the response variable (the variable whose reaction is traced).
- Positive response means movement above the baseline; negative response means movement below it.
- Observe how quickly the response decays or persists.
- Confidence intervals that include zero indicate that the response may not be statistically distinguishable from zero.

Orthogonalised IRFs can depend on variable ordering. State the identification approach used.

### Step 14: Conduct forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD)

**What we do**

Estimate the proportion of each variable's forecast error variance attributable to shocks in each variable in the system.

**Why we do it**

FEVD helps assess the relative contribution of own shocks and cross-variable shocks to forecast uncertainty.

**How to interpret**

- A high own-shock share means the variable's own innovations explain most of its forecast error variance.
- A larger contribution from another variable suggests greater cross-variable influence within the model.
- Contributions for a given variable and horizon sum to 100%.
- FEVD is conditional on the model and shock-identification method.

### Step 15: Generate VAR forecasts

**What we do**

Forecast all endogenous variables over a selected horizon and inspect forecast intervals or fan charts.

**Why we do it**

VAR can produce joint forecasts that account for the lagged interactions in the system.

**How to interpret**

Review the forecast direction, magnitude and uncertainty for each variable. Forecasts are conditional on the estimated model and the assumption that its relationships remain sufficiently stable.

## 2.3 VAR workflow summary

Prepare variables → Explore → Test stationarity → Transform if required → Align series → Select lags → Estimate VAR → Stability → Serial correlation/ARCH/normality diagnostics → Parameter stability → Granger causality → IRF → FEVD → Forecast.

# 3. VECM – Vector Error Correction Model

## 3.1 Objective

VECM is used when multiple non-stationary variables are integrated of the same order (commonly I(1)) and have one or more cointegrating relationships.

Cointegration means that although individual series may be non-stationary, a particular linear combination of them is stationary. This represents a long-run equilibrium relationship.

VECM combines:
- **Long-run equilibrium information:** captured by the error-correction term.
- **Short-run dynamics:** captured by lagged differences.

A simplified VECM representation is:

\[
\Delta Y_t = \alpha\beta'Y_{t-1}
+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\Gamma_i\Delta Y_{t-i}
+\varepsilon_t
\]

where:
- \(\beta'Y_{t-1}\) represents the cointegrating relationship(s).
- \(\alpha\) contains adjustment coefficients.
- \(\Gamma_i\) captures short-run dynamics.

Use VECM when the variables are non-stationary in levels, are commonly I(1), and cointegration is supported by an appropriate test.

## 3.2 Step-by-step methodology

### Step 1: Select variables based on the long-run research question

**What we do**

Select variables that have a theoretical or economic reason to share a long-run relationship.

**Why we do it**

Cointegration is not simply a statistical association. The variables should have a meaningful long-run relationship that can be justified by theory or the research question.

**How to interpret**

Explain why the variables may move together over the long run. Avoid including unrelated variables only because they are available.

### Step 2: Check data alignment and plot the level series

**What we do**

Prepare the level data, align dates, inspect missing values and plot each series.

**Why we do it**

The Johansen cointegration procedure is applied to the level series. Correct alignment and sensible data properties are essential.

**How to interpret**

Look for trends and broad co-movement. Similar plots alone do not prove cointegration.

### Step 3: Test the order of integration

**What we do**

Apply ADF/KPSS tests to each variable in levels and, where needed, first differences.

**Why we do it**

The standard Johansen VECM framework is commonly applied when variables are I(1): non-stationary in levels but stationary after first differencing.

**How to interpret**

- Level non-stationary and first difference stationary: evidence consistent with I(1).
- If variables have different integration orders, the standard I(1) Johansen setup may not be appropriate without further methodological justification.
- Do not proceed merely because the level series look trending.

### Step 4: Choose the lag structure and deterministic terms

**What we do**

Determine a suitable lag order for the underlying VAR in levels and choose the deterministic specification, such as an intercept or trend, according to the data and theory.

**Why we do it**

Johansen test results and cointegration rank can be sensitive to lag length and deterministic terms.

**How to interpret**

Report the chosen lag order and whether the model includes a constant and/or trend. Explain why the specification is appropriate. In common R implementations, the `K` argument in `ca.jo()` refers to the lag order of the VAR in levels, so check the package documentation and chosen convention carefully.

### Step 5: Perform the Johansen cointegration test

**What we do**

Use the Johansen trace test and/or maximum-eigenvalue test to determine the number of cointegrating relationships.

**Why we do it**

The test estimates the cointegration rank, which determines how many long-run equilibrium relationships are present.

**Hypotheses for the trace test**

At each rank r:

\[
H_0: \text{Cointegration rank is at most } r
\]

\[
H_1: \text{Cointegration rank is greater than } r
\]

**How to interpret**

- Begin with r = 0 (no cointegrating relationships).
- Compare the trace statistic with its critical value, or use the reported p-value where available.
- If r = 0 is rejected, move to r = 1.
- Continue until the null is not rejected.
- The sequential result indicates the estimated cointegration rank.

Do not interpret one comparison in isolation; report the sequential rank decision and the deterministic specification.

### Step 6: Decide whether VECM is appropriate

**What we do**

Use the cointegration rank and integration findings to determine whether a VECM is justified.

**Why we do it**

A VECM is appropriate when the system contains non-stationary I(1) variables with at least one cointegrating relationship.

**How to interpret**

- Rank r = 0: no cointegration detected; a differenced VAR may be appropriate for short-run dynamics.
- Rank greater than zero: estimate a VECM with the selected rank.
- Full rank generally indicates the level system is stationary under the model setup, so the usual I(1) cointegration interpretation needs reconsideration.

### Step 7: Estimate the VECM

**What we do**

Estimate the VECM using the selected cointegration rank and lag structure. In the R workflow, `cajorls()` can estimate the restricted short-run equations from a `ca.jo` result.

**Why we do it**

The model estimates both the short-run changes and the adjustment toward long-run equilibrium.

**How to interpret**

There are two main components:

1. **Error-correction term (ECT):** indicates the previous period's deviation from long-run equilibrium.
2. **Lagged differences:** describe short-run effects of changes in the variables.

### Step 8: Interpret the adjustment coefficients

**What we do**

Examine the coefficients on the error-correction term in each equation, including their signs and statistical significance.

**Why we do it**

The adjustment coefficients indicate which variables respond when the system deviates from long-run equilibrium.

**How to interpret**

- A negative and statistically significant adjustment coefficient is often consistent with correction toward equilibrium, depending on the normalisation and sign convention of the cointegrating vector.
- A larger absolute coefficient can indicate faster adjustment per observation period, subject to the model's parameterisation.
- An insignificant coefficient suggests that the variable does not show clear adjustment to disequilibrium in that equation.
- Do not automatically say every negative coefficient is stabilising; check the sign convention and the full equilibrium relation.

### Step 9: Interpret short-run coefficients

**What we do**

Review coefficients on lagged first differences.

**Why we do it**

These coefficients describe short-run effects among changes in the variables, separate from the long-run adjustment mechanism.

**How to interpret**

A significant coefficient on a lagged difference suggests a short-run predictive relationship conditional on the other included terms. Explain the sign, magnitude, lag and p-value.

### Step 10: Conduct VECM diagnostics

**What we do**

Check residual serial correlation, stability and other relevant residual properties.

**Why we do it**

Cointegration does not guarantee that the estimated dynamic model is adequately specified.

**How to interpret**

- Residual serial correlation suggests omitted dynamics or an inadequate lag structure.
- Instability may indicate changing relationships.
- Normality and heteroscedasticity results should be reported where relevant to the inference strategy.

### Step 11: Use VECM for dynamic analysis and forecasting

**What we do**

Where supported by the fitted model, examine dynamic responses and generate forecasts.

**Why we do it**

VECM forecasts preserve both short-run dynamics and the long-run cointegrating structure.

**How to interpret**

Forecasts should be considered jointly with the equilibrium relationship. Explain the forecast horizon, uncertainty and any transformation used to return to level values.

## 3.3 VAR versus VECM: choosing the correct model

| Situation | Common modelling choice |
|---|---|
| All variables are stationary in levels | VAR in levels |
| Variables are I(1), but no cointegration is found | VAR in first differences |
| Variables are I(1) and cointegrated | VECM |
| Variables have mixed integration orders or are I(2) | Requires a different or extended modelling strategy |

The choice depends on the integration properties, cointegration evidence, theoretical question and model assumptions.

## 3.4 VECM workflow summary

Select variables → Align and plot levels → Test integration order → Choose VAR lag/deterministic terms → Johansen test → Determine rank → Estimate VECM → Interpret ECT → Interpret short-run coefficients → Diagnostics → Dynamic analysis/forecast.

# 4. GARCH – Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity

## 4.1 Objective

GARCH models time-varying conditional variance (volatility). It is commonly used for financial returns, where periods of high volatility tend to cluster and periods of low volatility tend to cluster.

A basic GARCH(1,1) variance equation is:

\[
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2
\]

where:
- \(\sigma_t^2\) is conditional variance at time t.
- \(\omega\) is the constant term.
- \(\alpha\) measures the impact of recent shocks (ARCH effect).
- \(\beta\) measures volatility persistence (GARCH effect).
- \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) is the previous squared innovation.

Use GARCH when the research question concerns conditional volatility, volatility clustering, risk or the response of volatility to shocks.

## 4.2 Step-by-step methodology

### Step 1: Import and prepare the price data

**What we do**
- Import price/index data.
- Parse dates and convert prices to numeric.
- Sort observations chronologically.
- Remove or investigate missing and duplicate observations.
- Check that prices are positive before taking logarithms.

**Why we do it**

GARCH is generally fitted to a return or mean-equation residual series rather than directly to a trending price level.

**How to interpret**

Confirm the time frequency and sample period. For market data, make sure the series follows trading dates and that missing observations are not being mistaken for zero returns.

### Step 2: Plot the price and return series

**What we do**

Plot the price level and the calculated return series.

**Why we do it**

The price plot shows broad market movement; the return plot reveals short-term fluctuations and possible volatility clustering.

**How to interpret**

- Price levels may trend and are often non-stationary.
- Returns often fluctuate around a relatively stable mean.
- Clusters of large absolute returns suggest time-varying volatility.
- Large isolated movements may represent shocks or data errors and should be investigated.

### Step 3: Calculate returns

**What we do**

Calculate log returns:

\[
r_t = 100 \times [\log(P_t)-\log(P_{t-1})]
\]

Multiplication by 100 expresses returns in percentage points.

**Why we do it**

Financial returns are more suitable for volatility modelling than price levels. Log returns are additive over time and are commonly used in financial econometrics.

**How to interpret**

A return of 1.2 means an approximate 1.2% log return for that period when returns are multiplied by 100. Confirm whether the code uses decimal or percentage returns because this affects coefficient scales.

### Step 4: Examine descriptive statistics and distribution

**What we do**

Calculate mean, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis; inspect a histogram and/or density plot.

**Why we do it**

Financial returns may be skewed and have heavy tails compared with a normal distribution.

**How to interpret**

- Mean: average return over the sample.
- Standard deviation: unconditional dispersion of returns.
- Positive skewness: longer right tail.
- Negative skewness: longer left tail.
- High excess kurtosis: heavier tails and more extreme observations than a normal distribution.

Heavy tails may motivate Student-t or another non-normal innovation distribution.

### Step 5: Test stationarity of the return series

**What we do**

Use tests such as ADF and KPSS on returns.

**Why we do it**

The conditional mean and variance modelling framework generally assumes a suitably stationary return process.

**How to interpret**

Apply the same ADF and KPSS null-hypothesis logic described in the ARIMA section. If the returns are not stationary, investigate the data and mean specification before fitting GARCH.

### Step 6: Identify the mean equation

**What we do**

Specify the conditional mean, which may be a constant mean or an ARMA-type process depending on the autocorrelation structure.

**Why we do it**

GARCH models the conditional variance of innovations from the mean equation. If the mean dynamics are misspecified, residuals may retain serial dependence.

**How to interpret**

Inspect mean-equation coefficients and residual autocorrelation. The mean model and variance model serve different purposes: the former describes expected returns; the latter describes conditional risk.

### Step 7: Test for ARCH effects

**What we do**

Apply an ARCH-LM test to residuals from the mean equation.

**Why we do it**

The test checks whether past squared residuals help explain current squared residuals, which is evidence of conditional heteroscedasticity.

**Hypotheses**

\[
H_0: \text{No ARCH effects}
\]

\[
H_1: \text{ARCH effects are present}
\]

**How to interpret**

- p-value below 0.05: reject the null; evidence of ARCH effects, supporting a conditional volatility model.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null; insufficient evidence of ARCH effects at the tested lag order.

The test result depends on the residuals, lag order and mean specification.

### Step 8: Specify and estimate a GARCH model

**What we do**

Estimate a GARCH(1,1) model or other justified specification using a suitable package.

**Why we do it**

GARCH allows current conditional variance to depend on past shocks and past conditional variance.

**How to interpret**

- \(\omega\): baseline variance component; typically expected to be positive.
- \(\alpha\): reaction of volatility to recent news/shocks.
- \(\beta\): persistence of past conditional variance.
- \(\alpha+\beta\): often used as a summary of volatility persistence in a standard GARCH(1,1), subject to model assumptions.

A larger \(\alpha\) indicates stronger immediate response to shocks. A larger \(\beta\) indicates greater dependence on previous volatility.

### Step 9: Compare alternative GARCH specifications

**What we do**

Where justified, compare models such as GARCH, EGARCH or GJR-GARCH, and alternative innovation distributions such as normal or Student-t.

**Why we do it**

Financial volatility can be asymmetric: negative shocks may affect volatility differently from positive shocks. Alternative models can capture this behaviour.

**How to interpret**

- Standard GARCH: symmetric response to positive and negative shocks of equal magnitude.
- GJR-GARCH: includes an indicator term to allow negative shocks to have an additional effect.
- EGARCH: models log variance and can capture asymmetric responses without the same non-negativity constraints.
- Student-t innovations: allow heavier tails than the normal distribution.

Compare information criteria, coefficient significance and residual diagnostics rather than selecting a model by name alone.

### Step 10: Check parameter constraints and persistence

**What we do**

Review coefficient signs, significance and persistence measures.

**Why we do it**

A mathematically estimated model may still be poorly specified or violate conditions needed for a meaningful variance process.

**How to interpret**

For a conventional GARCH(1,1), typical restrictions include positive variance parameters and a persistence condition such as \(\alpha+\beta < 1\) for covariance stationarity. Boundary cases and alternative specifications require model-specific interpretation.

A value of \(\alpha+\beta\) close to 1 indicates highly persistent volatility shocks.

### Step 11: Diagnose standardised residuals

**What we do**

Inspect standardised residuals and their ACF; test for remaining serial correlation and remaining ARCH effects. Check the distributional assumptions as appropriate.

**Why we do it**

A good volatility model should remove predictable structure from the standardised residuals and their squares.

**How to interpret**

- Standardised residual ACF: checks remaining mean dependence.
- Squared standardised residual ACF: checks remaining volatility dependence.
- Ljung-Box test on standardised residuals: assesses residual serial correlation.
- ARCH-LM test on standardised residuals: assesses remaining ARCH effects.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the relevant null at the tested lags.
- p-value below 0.05: evidence of remaining structure; model may need revision.

Also inspect whether standardised residuals have heavy tails relative to the assumed innovation distribution.

### Step 12: Extract and plot conditional volatility

**What we do**

Extract the estimated conditional standard deviation or variance and plot it over time.

**Why we do it**

This provides a time-varying estimate of risk rather than one constant sample standard deviation.

**How to interpret**

- Peaks indicate periods of elevated estimated volatility.
- Troughs indicate relatively calm periods.
- Persistent high volatility indicates slow decay after shocks.
- Conditional standard deviation is in the same units as the modelled return; conditional variance is in squared units.

### Step 13: Forecast volatility

**What we do**

Generate conditional variance or volatility forecasts over the desired horizon.

**Why we do it**

Volatility forecasts can support risk management, portfolio allocation, option pricing and stress analysis.

**How to interpret**

Forecast volatility is conditional on the fitted model and available information. It is not a guaranteed future realised volatility value. Report the forecast horizon and units clearly.

### Step 14: Compare volatility across assets or periods

**What we do**

Estimate comparable models for different assets or periods, using consistent data definitions and settings where possible.

**Why we do it**

This enables comparison of conditional risk dynamics.

**How to interpret**

Compare:
- average conditional volatility,
- peak volatility,
- persistence,
- response to shocks,
- forecast volatility.

Ensure the return frequency, scaling, sample window and innovation assumptions are comparable.

## 4.3 Extensions: DCC-GARCH and BEKK-GARCH

### DCC-GARCH

**What it does**

Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC)-GARCH models time-varying conditional variances for multiple assets and allows their conditional correlations to change over time.

**Why we use it**

It is useful when the research question concerns changing co-movement or diversification benefits across assets.

**How to interpret**

- Conditional volatility describes each asset's time-varying risk.
- Conditional correlation describes how the relationship between pairs of assets changes over time.
- Rising correlation can reduce diversification benefits.
- Correlation is not the same as causality.

### BEKK-GARCH

**What it does**

BEKK is a multivariate GARCH specification that models conditional variance and covariance dynamics while imposing a parameterisation designed to help ensure a positive-definite covariance matrix.

**Why we use it**

It can be used to study volatility transmission and cross-market volatility interactions.

**How to interpret**

Interpret own-market and cross-market ARCH/GARCH effects according to the exact BEKK parameterisation. Do not label a coefficient as volatility spillover without checking the relevant equation and restrictions. BEKK coefficients are matrix-based, so interpretation is more involved than in a univariate GARCH model.

## 4.4 GARCH workflow summary

Price data → Calculate returns → Explore distribution → Test stationarity → Specify mean equation → ARCH-LM test → Estimate GARCH family → Compare specifications → Check persistence/constraints → Standardised residual diagnostics → Conditional volatility plot → Volatility forecast → Cross-asset extensions if required.

---

# 5. How the four methods differ

| Method | Main purpose | Data structure | Main question |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARIMA | Univariate forecasting | One time series | How can the past of this series help forecast its future? |
| VAR | Short-run multivariate dynamics | Multiple stationary series | How do variables influence one another through their lags? |
| VECM | Short-run dynamics plus long-run equilibrium | Multiple cointegrated I(1) series | How do variables adjust after deviating from long-run equilibrium? |
| GARCH | Conditional volatility modelling | Usually returns or mean-equation residuals | How does volatility change over time and respond to shocks? |

## 5.1 Choosing the method

- Choose **ARIMA** when the focus is forecasting one variable.
- Choose **VAR** when the focus is short-run interactions among multiple stationary variables.
- Choose **VECM** when multiple I(1) variables are cointegrated and both long-run equilibrium and short-run adjustment matter.
- Choose **GARCH** when the focus is time-varying volatility, volatility clustering or financial risk.
- A study can use more than one method if each method addresses a distinct research question and the sequence is methodologically justified.

# 6. Common interpretation mistakes to avoid

1. **ADF and KPSS have opposite null hypotheses.** Do not interpret their p-values in the same way.
2. **A high R-squared does not establish a good time-series model.** Residual diagnostics and forecast validation are also necessary.
3. **Lower AIC/BIC is a relative comparison, not proof of model adequacy.**
4. **In-sample accuracy is not out-of-sample forecasting performance.**
5. **Granger causality is predictive precedence, not structural causality.**
6. **IRF interpretation depends on shock identification and, for orthogonalised IRFs, potentially variable ordering.**
7. **Cointegration is a property of a long-run linear combination, not simply two trending series moving together visually.**
8. **A VECM requires a justified integration and cointegration framework.**
9. **GARCH models conditional variance, not the price level itself.**
10. **A significant ARCH-LM result supports conditional heteroscedasticity but does not identify the best GARCH specification by itself.**
11. **A non-significant diagnostic test means failure to reject its null, not proof that the model is perfect.**
12. **Always state the data frequency, sample period, transformations, lag order, deterministic terms, innovation distribution and forecast horizon.**

# 7. Suggested structure for an academic methodology write-up

For each model, document the following:

1. **Research objective:** What relationship or forecasting problem is being studied?
2. **Data and variables:** Sources, frequency, sample period and definitions.
3. **Pre-processing:** Missing values, transformations, alignment and outlier treatment.
4. **Pre-estimation tests:** Stationarity, integration order and ARCH effects where relevant.
5. **Model specification:** ARIMA order, VAR lag order, VECM rank/deterministic terms or GARCH family/innovation distribution.
6. **Estimation:** Software, package, estimator and model settings.
7. **Diagnostics:** Tests performed, null hypotheses, statistics and p-values.
8. **Results:** Coefficients, signs, magnitudes, uncertainty and economic meaning.
9. **Forecast or dynamic analysis:** Holdout design, IRF, FEVD or volatility forecasts as relevant.
10. **Limitations:** Sample size, structural breaks, omitted variables, parameter instability and model assumptions.

**Final principle:** A sound time-series analysis is not just about fitting a model. It requires a clear research question, appropriate transformations, justified model selection, diagnostic checking and careful interpretation of what the model can—and cannot—establish.
