INTERPRETATION NOTES – NORMAL TIME SERIES ANALYSIS
--------------------------------------------------
- A time plot is an exploratory tool. Look for trend, seasonality, structural changes, outliers and changing variance.
- A log transformation is suitable only when observations are positive. Differences in log values approximate proportional/percentage changes for small movements.
- In additive decomposition, observed = trend + seasonal + irregular. In multiplicative decomposition, observed = trend × seasonal × irregular.
- A seasonal index above the additive baseline indicates a month/period that tends to be above the trend; below the baseline indicates a negative seasonal contribution.
- Moving averages smooth short-term fluctuations. A longer window produces a smoother series but reacts more slowly to changes.
- Least-squares trend estimates a fitted deterministic trend over the sample. Semi-averages provide a simple two-period trend approximation.
- `skewness()` describes asymmetry: positive values indicate right-skew; negative values indicate left-skew.
- `kurtosis()` interpretation depends on the package's definition. For `moments::kurtosis`, a value around 3 is approximately normal-like; above 3 is leptokurtic and below 3 is platykurtic.
- Do not infer stationarity from a plot alone. Use formal tests such as ADF and KPSS in the ARIMA/VAR sections.

### Performing Stationary test

$$
\begin{aligned}
  H_0 &: \text{The time series is non-stationary (contains a unit root)} \\
  H_1 &: \text{The time series is stationary}
\end{aligned}
$$

```{r}
adf.test(nft_y)
```


INTERPRETATION:
| Test | p-value | Decision |
|---|---:|---|
| ADF | Below 0.05 | Reject unit-root null; evidence supports stationarity |
| ADF | Above 0.05 | Fail to reject unit-root null; insufficient evidence of stationarity |
| KPSS | Below 0.05 | Reject stationarity null; evidence supports non-stationarity |
| KPSS | Above 0.05 | Fail to reject stationarity null |

The ADF and KPSS tests have opposite null hypotheses. Reading them together provides a more informative assessment. Results can sometimes be inconclusive or conflicting.

#### Interpretation:

- As the p-value is greater than 0.05, we fail to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the data is non stationary.

```{r}
kpss.test(nft_y)
```

#### Interpretation:

- Given the p-value is less than 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that data is non stationary.

INTERPRETATION:
- **ACF:** Shows correlation between the series and its lagged values.
- **PACF:** Shows the relationship with a particular lag after controlling for shorter lags.
- A PACF cut-off can suggest an AR component.
- An ACF cut-off can suggest an MA component.
- Gradual decay can indicate mixed ARMA behaviour.

These are identification clues, not rigid rules. Consider multiple candidate models.

### Performing Johansen's cointegration test

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_0&: \text{ There are no cointegration relationships}\\
H_1&: \text{ There are cointegration relationships}
\end{aligned}
$$

```{r}

INTERPRETATION:
- Begin with r = 0 (no cointegrating relationships).
- Compare the trace statistic with its critical value, or use the reported p-value where available.
- If r = 0 is rejected, move to r = 1.
- Continue until the null is not rejected.
- The sequential result indicates the estimated cointegration rank.

Do not interpret one comparison in isolation; report the sequential rank decision and the deterministic specification.

## Assuming lag as 2

jo_test <- ca.jo(sec_z,
                 type = "trace",
                 ecdet ="trend",
                 K = 2)
```

### Checking the summary

```{r}
summary(jo_test)
```

#### Interpretation:

* We can observe that when r = 0, the test statistic(**97.99**) \> (**87.31**) so we reject the null hypothesis and hereby conclude that there is at-least one cointegrating relationship. Checking further values of r, we can conclude that there is atmost one cointegrating relationship. Even if the sectors show short term relationship between them, there can be mild long run relationship between the sectors.

### Performing VECM test

```{r}
vecm_model <- cajorls(jo_test, r =1)
summary(vecm_model$rlm)
```

INTERPRETATION:
There are two main components:

1. **Error-correction term (ECT):** indicates the previous period's deviation from long-run equilibrium.
2. **Lagged differences:** describe short-run effects of changes in the variables.

1. DATA IMPORT, CLEANING AND PRICE PLOTS
- Confirm that the date index is correctly parsed, observations are chronological, and price columns are numeric.
- Check that all prices are positive before calculating log returns.
- Remove or investigate missing values; do not silently remove large blocks of data without checking their cause.

2. RAW-SERIES STATIONARITY
- ADF null hypothesis: the series has a unit root (non-stationary).
- ADF p-value below 0.05: reject the unit-root null; evidence supports stationarity.
- ADF p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the unit-root null; insufficient evidence of stationarity.
- Price/index levels are often non-stationary. This does not by itself mean the return series is non-stationary.

3. RETURN SERIES
- `diff(log(price))` gives continuously compounded returns; multiply by 100 if percentage units are preferred.
- Log returns are approximately percentage changes for small movements.
- Confirm that all return series use the same dates before combining them.

4. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS AND DISTRIBUTION
- Positive skewness indicates a longer/right tail; negative skewness indicates a longer/left tail.
- Kurtosis above 3 (under the conventional Pearson definition) indicates heavier tails than a normal distribution; below 3 indicates lighter tails.
- Financial returns often exhibit fat tails, so a normality rejection is common and can motivate a Student-t innovation distribution.
- Check the package's kurtosis convention before interpreting the benchmark value.

5. RETURN-SERIES STATIONARITY
- ADF p-value below 0.05 supports stationarity of returns.
- A stationary mean does not imply constant variance. Volatility can still change over time.

6. VOLATILITY CLUSTERING
- Volatility clustering means large absolute movements tend to be followed by large movements, and small movements by small movements.
- A return plot can reveal clustering, but formal ARCH testing is needed to assess conditional heteroskedasticity.

7. ARCH EFFECT TEST
- ARCH-LM null hypothesis: no ARCH effects (no conditional heteroskedasticity up to the tested lag order).
- p-value below 0.05: reject the null; evidence of ARCH effects. A conditional-volatility model may be appropriate.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null; the test does not detect ARCH effects at the chosen lag order.

8. SERIAL CORRELATION TEST
- Ljung-Box null hypothesis: no autocorrelation up to the selected lag.
- p-value above 0.05: fail to reject the null; no significant serial correlation detected at the tested lags.
- p-value below 0.05: evidence of remaining serial correlation. Consider whether the mean equation needs ARMA terms.
- Test returns and standardized residuals separately; they answer different diagnostic questions.

9. NORMALITY TEST
- Jarque-Bera null hypothesis: skewness and kurtosis are consistent with normality.
- p-value below 0.05: reject normality.
- Non-normal returns do not automatically rule out GARCH. Consider an appropriate innovation distribution, such as Student-t, and inspect standardized residuals.

10. sGARCH(1,1) MODEL
- The conditional variance equation includes a constant, a lagged squared innovation (ARCH effect) and a lagged conditional variance (GARCH effect).
- `alpha1` measures the response of volatility to recent shocks.
- `beta1` measures volatility persistence.
- `alpha1 + beta1` close to 1 indicates highly persistent volatility; a value below 1 is commonly associated with covariance-stationary variance under standard conditions.
- Check parameter constraints and convergence before interpreting estimates.
- A significant ARCH parameter indicates that recent shocks help explain current volatility; a significant GARCH parameter indicates persistence.

11. MEAN EQUATION
- ARMA terms model serial dependence in the conditional mean.
- Compare mean specifications using diagnostics and information criteria, not just coefficient significance.
- The mean equation and variance equation serve different purposes.

12. MODEL DIAGNOSTICS
- Standardized residuals should have no substantial remaining autocorrelation.
- Ljung-Box test on standardized residuals: p-value above 0.05 is consistent with no detected serial correlation.
- ARCH-LM test on standardized residuals: p-value above 0.05 is consistent with no remaining ARCH effect.
- Inspect residual ACF and diagnostic plots as well as test p-values.
- A good volatility model should capture conditional heteroskedasticity without leaving substantial structure in standardized residuals.

13. VOLATILITY FORECASTING
- `ugarchforecast()` produces forecasts of conditional mean and/or conditional variance.
- The conditional sigma forecast represents the modelled volatility, not the forecasted asset price.
- Volatility forecasts are conditional on the fitted model and generally revert toward the model's long-run variance when the model is mean-reverting.
- Clearly state the forecast horizon and the units used for returns/volatility.

14. EGARCH AND GJR-GARCH
- EGARCH models log conditional variance and can capture asymmetric responses to positive and negative shocks.
- GJR-GARCH adds an asymmetric term so negative and positive shocks can have different volatility effects.
- In these models, interpret the asymmetry parameter according to the exact parameterisation and software output; do not assume the sign has the same meaning across every implementation.
- Compare alternative specifications using information criteria, convergence, parameter plausibility and standardized-residual diagnostics.

15. DCC-GARCH
- DCC-GARCH models time-varying conditional correlations between multiple return series.
- `rcor()` extracts dynamic conditional correlations; `rcov()` extracts conditional covariance matrices.
- Correlations near +1 indicate strong positive co-movement; near -1 indicate strong negative co-movement; near 0 indicates weak linear co-movement.
- A changing correlation series indicates that diversification relationships vary over time.
- DCC estimates are conditional on the chosen univariate volatility specifications and model assumptions.

16. BEKK-GARCH
- BEKK models conditional covariance dynamics and can represent volatility and shock spillovers across series.
- Interpret the estimated matrices, t-statistics and p-values according to the package's parameterisation.
- A statistically significant off-diagonal element may provide evidence of cross-market shock/volatility transmission within the fitted model.
- Statistical significance is not, by itself, proof of economic causality.
